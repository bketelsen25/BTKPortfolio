# Major Assignment 1 Author’s Statement

By Brendan Ketelsen

My network map is a representation of my workplace experience with the SeaWorld IT department. I believe I included all the essential elements of this workplace's ecology, and the only things I chose not to incorporate were technologies used very rarely. The map consists of three different categories, separated by their different colors. Orange represents people or groups of people that make up the human portion of the information ecology. Green represents technologies that make up the other half of the ecology, connected to the people or other technologies based on how they interact. I also chose to include some categories that denote the uses of these technologies, represented in blue. I believe this helps put the technologies into groups with similar purposes, even if the functions of those technologies are completely different from each other. The only technologies that are not connected to one of these blue categories are Microsoft Teams, our main communication method, and Jira, our support ticket request system. This is because they are mainly used (in the case of Teams) to connect people together, or (in the case of Jira) to connect people with their technological systems by creating work requests. These two systems are very central in the diagram, which I believe accurately reflects how essential they are to the ecology as a whole. 

The concept of information ecologies is described as “a system of people, practices, values, and technologies in a particular local environment” (Nardi & O’Day, 1999). This ecology fits that definition pretty perfectly in my opinion, since that is exactly how the office operates: human techs and digital systems working together to keep a park up and running for thousands of guests, and neither could do that job without support from the other. The text goes on to state that “In information ecologies, the spotlight is not on technology, but on human activities that are served by technology” (Nardi & O’Day, 1999), which I believe is a great way to encompass how my ecology works. 
	
The technologies I included in my map are spread roughly 50-50 between the technician team and the infrastructure team. The technician team, which I am a part of, handles more of the office-based jobs such as managing employee computer access in Active Directory, PowerShell scripting to make some of our routine tasks quicker, and keeping inventory of our hardware (computers, printers, switches, registers, etc.). The infrastructure team handles more of the physical infrastructure out in the park, such as running or replacing cables, or installing and maintaining devices such as security cameras and Wi-Fi access points. Some of these technologies are also used interchangeably by both teams, such as PuTTY. This software is used for creating and changing the configurations of network switches, which are the foundation of a wired network in any environment. The infrastructure team uses it often since they deal more commonly with the physical installation and maintenance of switches, but the technician team is also able to use it to access and reconfigure switch ports if necessary, without even needing to see the physical switch. 

Four of the nodes on the network map have a thick black outline, denoting that they are a keystone species within the ecology. This means that they are “crucial to the shape and stability of the system” (Nardi & O’Day, 1999), and the ecology would collapse if any of the four of them were removed. As mentioned before, Teams facilitates nearly all communication both within our team and with other park employees, and Jira allows us to receive work requests from around the park. Without either of these systems, communication about our work with other areas of the park would be extremely impractical, and receiving work requests would be impossible. The technician team and infrastructure team are also highlighted because they compose the entire workforce of the IT team. The teams are distinct because two separate skill sets are required for the work of each team. This means that even if one team were removed and the other remained, they would not have the skills or the manpower to do the jobs of both teams at once. If both teams were removed, there would be no workforce, and the ecology would cease to exist.

Some parts of the ecology are connected with dotted lines rather than solid ones. This dotted line denotes a weak relationship, rather than the strong relationships with solid lines. The strength of a tie between people is a “combination of the amount of time of the emotional intensity, the intimacy (mutual confiding), and the reciprocal services which characterize the tie” (Rheingold, 2012). I added weak ties between the IT Director and the infrastructure and technician teams because he occasionally has meetings with certain team members or assigns leaders for specific projects, but his direct interactions with us are rather infrequent, and we typically only hear from him indirectly through our supervisor. The strong and weak tie definition isn’t exactly the same for ties between technologies, but I interpreted it as an infrequent or secondary interaction between the two systems. I placed weak ties from cabling to cameras and APs because both of those require small individual cable runs, but they make up only a small part of the overall amount of cabling work that is needed around the park. I also placed one between scripting and Active Directory because some of the scripts we use are intended to simplify processes within Active Directory, such as creating new user accounts or adding software licenses, but this is only a fraction of all our scripts, and Active Directory does not rely on the scripts to fulfill its purpose. 

I enjoyed creating this diagram because it helped me get a more visual idea of the structure of my workplace. I liked seeing how central some nodes ended up being, showing how many different areas of the office rely on systems like Jira or Teams, whereas systems that I very commonly use, like Active Directory, ended up being much more peripheral and having less of a crucial role in the ecology overall. This map was a great way to visualize the idea of information ecologies using an environment I interact with all the time, which definitely helped me solidify the concept better than just reading it in the book.
